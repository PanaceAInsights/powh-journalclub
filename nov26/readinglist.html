Hand eczema—Part 1: Epidemiology, pathogenesis, diagnosis, and work-up.

Bissonnette R, Agner T, Molin S, Guttman-Yassky E
J Am Acad Dermatol. 2025;93(5):1201-1210 · 10 pp
Eczema / ADContact / occupational

Hand eczema (HE), also referred to as hand dermatitis, is a frequent medical condition that can have an important negative impact on quality of life. Occupational HE is an important cause of medical disability. Multiple inflammatory pathways are upregulated, and barrier genes are downregulated in HE. Atopic dermatitis and wet work are 2 important risk factors for HE. Clinical presentation can be variable and location may comprise palmar and dorsal aspects, fingers, fingertips, and wrists of one or both hands. Work-up is directed at identifying causative factors and excluding other diagnoses. The first article of this CME series focuses on epidemiology, etiopathogenesis, differential diagnosis, work-up, and patch testing.

Establishment of standardized definitions and a core set of outcome characteristics following hidradenitis suppurativa surgery developed by an expert Delphi consensus.

Westerkam LL, van der Zee HH, Bechara FG, Goldberg S, Jemec GB, Caffrey J, … Sayed CJ
J Am Acad Dermatol. 2025;93(6):1499-1508 · 10 pp
HidradenitisProcedural / cosmeticOutcomes / QoL
Background

Hidradenitis suppurativa (HS) is a chronic inflammatory skin condition often requiring surgical intervention for definitive treatment. Previous studies evaluated postsurgical outcomes, but no standardization exists for collection and nomenclature for HS surgical outcomes.

Objective

To characterize and define surgical outcome terminology.

Methods

A modified Delphi protocol was used to reach consensus on data to collect and terms to describe outcomes following HS surgical procedures. A five-member steering committee created preliminary definitions and surveys, which were distributed via Qualtrics to a group of international HS experts. A nine-point Likert scale was used, and a score of at least 7 was needed for an item to reach agreement.

Results

Twenty-five dermatologists and general and plastic surgeons participated in the Delphi study. Following 2 rounds of surveys and feedback, the consensus terminology to describe outcomes included surgical site and regional persistence and progression. Consensus was also reached on key features to report as part of each outcome.

Limitations

Limitations include narrow scope and small number of participants from limited geographical areas.

Conclusion

Surgery persistence and progression definitions were agreed upon by a group of international HS experts. This consensus is a first step toward standardizing terminology and reporting for HS surgical outcomes.

Skincare ingredients recommended by cosmetic dermatologists: A Delphi consensus study.

Alvarez GV, Kang BY, Richmond AM, Hoss E, Sulewski R, Minkis K, … Alam M
J Am Acad Dermatol. 2025;93(6):1509-1525 · 17 pp
Procedural / cosmeticAcnePigmentary
Background

There is ambiguity regarding the topical cosmetic ingredients preferred for common skin complaints.

Objective

To determine which topical ingredients are frequently recommended by cosmetic dermatologists for fine lines and wrinkles, acne, redness, dark spots, large pores, dry skin, and oily skin.

Methods

Literature review to develop long list of ingredients. Reduced by expert panel to most salient ingredients. Two rounds of Delphi consensus survey with second expert panel of clinicians and teachers. Comparative literature review to summarize published evidence supporting each consensus ingredient.

Results

List of 318 ingredients reduced by a panel of 17 dermatologists to 83. Two Delphi rounds completed by 62 dermatologists at 43 centers. Most consensus ingredients supported by level 1b or 2b evidence.

Limitations

Some ingredients based on expert opinion.

Conclusion

Consensus exists among expert cosmetic dermatologists regarding ingredients most useful for common dermatologic concerns.

Establishing consensus on defining failure of topical therapy in psoriasis: Recommendations from the International Psoriasis Council.

Strober BE, Blauvelt A, van de Kerkhof PCM, Asawanonda P, Chandra R, Gonzalez-Cantero A, … Skov L
J Am Acad Dermatol. 2026;94(1):372-375 · 4 pp
PsoriasisPractice & policy
Synopsis · compiled from the full text

Health-policy statement (open access) from the International Psoriasis Council's Disease Severity Working Group, building on its two-category severity scheme, in which failure of topical therapy is one of three criteria for systemic treatment alongside body surface area (BSA) above 10% and high-impact sites. Tables of national guidelines and expert groups show that most cite topical failure as a reason to escalate without defining it, and none sets a minimum duration or number of topical courses. Survey data (UPLIFT) point to undertreatment: over half of patients with less than 3% BSA rated their psoriasis moderate or severe yet had only topical or no treatment. Proposed definition: failure is not reaching clear or nearly clear skin (BSA 1% or less, or PGA 0/1) after two consecutive 4-week courses of guideline-based topical therapy, with strong weight given to patients who rate their disease moderate or severe despite a mild clinician assessment and who report significant physical, psychological or social impact. Potent corticosteroids should not run continuously beyond 4 weeks (super-potent, 2-4 weeks), and failure in that window should prompt phototherapy or systemic therapy, with topicals kept as adjuncts. Where systemic therapy is inaccessible, tailored topical therapy remains reasonable. It follows the council's severity-reclassification update in the October edition.

Review JAAD 94(1) · January 2026 · Clinical ReviewNew this month

Low-dose naltrexone for treatment of dermatologic conditions: A clinical review.

Zhou MH, Elston DM, Morrison BW, Lipner SR
J Am Acad Dermatol. 2026;94(1):200-207 · 8 pp
TherapeuticsPruritus

Naltrexone is a long-acting opioid receptor antagonist that may be compounded at lower doses (1-5 mg) to treat inflammatory or autoimmune conditions. Low-dose naltrexone (LDN) transiently blocks the opioid receptor, resulting in increased ligand and receptor expression, favoring κ over μ receptors and decreasing inflammatory mediators and itch. In addition, LDN antagonizes nonopioid receptors, including the pro-inflammatory toll-like receptor 4. Altogether, the anti-inflammatory properties of LDN have supported its use in dermatologic conditions, including Hailey-Hailey disease, Darier disease, pruritus, lichen planus, psoriasis, dermatomyositis, hidradenitis suppurativa, and body-focused repetition behaviors. LDN has demonstrated efficacy in treating these dermatologic conditions with improvement in disease severity, body surface area involvement, and associated symptoms. However, clinical response may vary based on initial disease presentation. There is also evidence supporting LDN in combination with other anti-inflammatory agents. Fewer side effects have been reported with LDN compared to standard naltrexone doses, though larger studies are required to optimize dosage and management. LDN is commonly compounded by a pharmacy, which may pose barriers to access. In this clinical review, we describe updated and practical uses of LDN in inflammatory dermatologic conditions, associated side effects, cost barriers, and comparisons to standard naltrexone doses.

Glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonists in the adjunctive treatment of patients with hidradenitis suppurativa: A systematic review.

Sánchez-Feliciano A, Herrera H, Charrow A, Barbieri JS
J Am Acad Dermatol. 2025;93(5):1334-1337 · 4 pp
HidradenitisTherapeutics
Synopsis · compiled from the full text

Systematic review (PRISMA; risk of bias rated with the Murad tool for case reports and case series) of glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonists (GLP-1RAs) in hidradenitis suppurativa (HS). PubMed and Embase were searched to 31 October 2024. Six studies were included: two retrospective cohorts (45 and 30 patients, semaglutide), one prospective cohort (14 patients, liraglutide) and three case reports, 92 patients in all, every one with obesity and/or diabetes (mean age 42.3 years; 80.4% female). Semaglutide accounted for 81.5% of patients, liraglutide 17.4% and tirzepatide 1%, for 1 to 36 months (median 5.6). Most patients stayed on stable HS therapy, biologics included. Overall, 58.6% showed significant improvement in lesion burden, and mean DLQI fell from 13.05 to 9.19 in the studies that reported it. In the larger semaglutide cohort, higher doses tracked with improvement at 3, 6 and 12 months whether or not patients had diabetes; the liraglutide cohort reported falls in Hurley stage, DLQI and pain. The authors stress that observational designs preclude causal inference and call for prospective trials.

Systematic review JAAD 93(6) · December 2025 · Brief ReportsBrief reportNew this month

Novel application of punch excision therapy in the treatment of keloids: A systematic review.

Bai JQA, Ogawa R, Lee YI, Kim J, Liu C
J Am Acad Dermatol. 2025;93(6):1537-1540 · 4 pp
Procedural / cosmeticTherapeutics
Synopsis · compiled from the full text

PROSPERO-registered systematic review (CRD42024589180) of punch excision for keloids. MEDLINE, Embase, CENTRAL and Scopus were searched in July 2024; of 228 records, 7 studies with 248 patients were included (mean age 29.1 years; 62.9% female): 2 randomized trials, 2 retrospective studies, 2 case series and 1 self-controlled trial, all from China, Korea or Japan. Every study combined punch excision with intralesional steroid. The techniques fall into three groups: drill (circumferential perforations around the keloid), side insertion (lateral dissection with a fine needle) and core excision (removing the cylindrical core with a 3-5 mm punch, used for ear keloids). Across the 4 studies (196 patients) reporting the Vancouver Scar Scale, scores fell by a mean of 63.7%. Core excision in 22 patients with ear keloids gave a Physician's Global Assessment of 1 in all, and more than 50% reduction in height and volume in 95%. Four studies reported no recurrence; three reported 5% to 22% at 12 to 24 months. Adverse events were minor: bleeding, pain and dyspigmentation, with menstrual disturbance reported in two trials.

Appraisal note · from the full text

Every included patient was Asian, and protocols, sites and scar scales varied. The text and Table I attach the 22% and 12.5% recurrence rates to different techniques (the text says drill and side insertion respectively; the table gives 22% for the side-insertion study and 12.5% for a drill study), so check the table before quoting them.

Cutaneous manifestations of tear gas exposure: A systematic review.

Gratz B, Shahrour N, Shahrour L, Dalia Y
J Am Acad Dermatol. 2025;93(6):1553-1555 · 3 pp

Synopsis · compiled from the full text

Systematic review (open access) of the skin effects of tear gas agents: chloroacetophenone (CN), 2-chlorobenzylidene malononitrile (CS) and oleoresin capsicum (OC, pepper spray). MEDLINE, Embase and Web of Science were searched in November 2024 for clinical studies, case reports and case series, screened by two independent reviewers. Systematic review of off-label upadacitinib, a JAK1-selective inhibitor, for refractory dermatomyositis. PubMed, Embase, Cochrane and Scopus were searched; 47 articles were screened and 6 included (case reports and case series, graded with the 2009 Oxford levels of evidence), giving 15 patients with a mean age of 56.3 years (range 31-86). The evidence is case-level only, and every included case reported improvement. The text says 12 patients were male and 3 female, but Table I and the patient-level table give 3 men and 12 women.

Topical estrogen for skin aging: A systematic review of safety and efficacy.

Farkas E, Goldblatt A, Nehorayan I, Lefkowitz RB, Fleshner L, Tepper K, Marmon S
J Am Acad Dermatol. 2026;94(1):212-215 · 4 pp
Procedural / cosmeticDrug safety
Synopsis · compiled from the full text

PRISMA-guided systematic review of topical estrogens for skin aging, prompted by menopause-targeted marketing (search strategy and study-level data in the online supplement). Estradiol (E2) was the active agent in 10 trials, mostly small prospective studies with only one double-blind randomized trial; skin thickness improved in 4 of 5 and collagen in 5 of 5 trials that measured them, but gynecologic effects were not assessed. Estriol (E3; one double-blind trial and two open-label trials, all against 0.01% E2, up to 6 months) gave broadly similar gains in wrinkles, firmness and hydration. Phytoestrogens (3 small double-blind trials) raised thickness, collagen and hyaluronic acid but less than E2. Methyl-estradiol-propanoate improved aging markers in 3 pilot studies; conjugated equine estrogen thickened skin in one placebo-controlled study but shifted the vaginal maturation index; estrone 1% failed to improve wrinkles or elasticity and raised MMP-1 about tenfold. Breast tension with E2, E3 and conjugated estrogen, and a prolactin rise in one study, point to systemic absorption, and hyperpigmentation was seen with E2 and E3. The authors warn against the social-media trend of applying prescription vaginal estrogen to the face: no trial assessed endometrial safety, so women with a uterus not taking a progestogen may risk hyperplasia. Heterogeneity ruled out meta-analysis.

Primary cutaneous nocardiosis: A systematic review.

Xu Z, Bao F, Liu H, Zhang F
J Am Acad Dermatol. 2026;94(1):232-233 · 2 pp
Infection
Synopsis · compiled from the full text

Systematic review of primary cutaneous nocardiosis, searching PubMed and two Chinese databases (Wanfang Data and the Full-text Database of Chinese Medical Journals) to February 2025: 357 cases from 257 articles across 28 countries (56% Asia, 27% North America, 12% Europe). Mean age was 48.5 years (6 days to 89 years), 62% were male, and 70% of recorded occupations were agricultural. Lesions sat mostly on the lower (45%) and upper (39%) limbs, typically as nodules, ulcers and abscesses. Linezolid, minocycline, amikacin and TMP-SMX each had susceptibility above 90%, whereas most isolates resisted penicillins (92%) and ciprofloxacin (68%), with cephalosporins and imipenem near 50%. Outcomes: 59% healed, 25% improved, 1.7% died and 14% unknown.

Adverse events of topical treatments for rosacea: A systematic review of randomized controlled trials.

Bai JQA, Geng RSQ, Akuffo-Addo E, Lam M, Sibbald C
J Am Acad Dermatol. 2026;94(1):261-264 · 4 pp
RosaceaDrug safety
Synopsis · compiled from the full text

Systematic review (open access; PRISMA; MEDLINE and Embase; two independent screeners) of treatment-emergent adverse events in randomized trials of the FDA-approved topical rosacea treatments. Thirty-one trials covered 13,459 patients (mean age 50.3 years; 71.4% female; mean treatment 21 weeks). Relative risks against placebo were computed for each event, leaving out zero-event outcomes. Ivermectin 1% (21% of patients) showed no event above placebo: its largest relative risks were all below 1 (myalgia 0.88, dryness 0.75, burning 0.69). Azelaic acid 15-20% raised stinging (RR 3.08), pruritus (2.69) and pain (2.64); encapsulated benzoyl peroxide 5% raised pruritus (2.88), erythema and pain (2.64 each); oxymetazoline raised rosacea worsening (RR 6.90; 2.1% of treated patients) and contact dermatitis (2.97); minocycline 1.5-3% raised pruritus (1.91); metronidazole's top signals (nasopharyngitis RR 3, contact dermatitis RR 2) rest on small numbers; sulfur/sodium sulfacetamide, tested in only 38 patients, raised erythema (RR 3.82). Head to head, ivermectin caused less burning (RR 0.37) and dryness (0.21) than azelaic acid. The authors conclude topical ivermectin is the best tolerated option.

Appraisal note · from the full text

Relative risks come from pooled raw counts without confidence intervals, and Table I gives a number needed to harm even where the relative risk is below 1 (for example ivermectin burning, RR 0.69, NNH 122). Events are treatment-emergent, not necessarily treatment-related.

Diagnostic accuracy of ChatGPT in dermatology: A meta-analysis of textual versus visual prompts.

AI / informaticsSkin cancer
